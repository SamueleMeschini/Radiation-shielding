Physical model and equations
\[\mu(E)=\rho\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)(E)\]

Here \(\mu\) is the linear attenuation coefficient, \(\rho\) is density, and \(\mu/\rho\) is the mass attenuation coefficient at photon energy \(E\).

\[m_A=\rho x,\qquad \tau=\mu x=\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)m_A\]

The physical thickness is \(x\), \(m_A\) is areal density, and \(\tau\) is optical thickness.

\[T_{\mathrm{unc}}(x)=e^{-\mu x},\qquad \mathrm{HVL}=\frac{\ln 2}{\mu},\qquad \mathrm{TVL}=\frac{\ln 10}{\mu}\]

The uncollided transmission, half-value layer, and tenth-value layer describe photons that do not interact.

\[T_N=\frac{J_{N,\mathrm{out}}}{J_{N,\mathrm{in}}},\qquad T_E=\frac{J_{E,\mathrm{out}}}{E_0J_{N,\mathrm{in}}}\]

\(T_N\) is the number of photons crossing the downstream surface per emitted source photon. \(T_E\) is the transmitted photon energy divided by the initial photon energy.

\[\overline{E}_{\mathrm{out}}=E_0\frac{T_E}{T_N},\qquad B_N=\frac{T_N}{T_{\mathrm{unc}}},\qquad B_E=\frac{T_E}{T_{\mathrm{unc}}}\]

\(\overline{E}_{\mathrm{out}}\) is the mean transmitted photon energy. \(B_N\) and \(B_E\) are specific to this geometry and tally response; they are not universal material properties.

Compare materials

Shielding materials can be compared using physical thickness, mass per unit area, or number of mean free paths. These comparisons answer different engineering questions. Equal physical thickness emphasizes compactness, while equal areal density compares shields having the same mass per unit area. Equal areal density does not imply equal attenuation because the mass attenuation coefficient depends on material composition and photon energy.

How to read this comparison

Equal physical thickness compares compactness. Equal areal density compares mass per area, while the mass attenuation coefficient still depends on material and energy. Equal optical thickness gives equal uncollided attenuation.

Photon interactions

The relative importance of coherent scattering, Compton scattering, photoelectric absorption, and pair production changes with photon energy and material composition. The plotted quantities describe the probability that the first interaction is of each type. They do not describe the final fate of the complete photon history, which may contain several successive interactions.

Compton and pair-production equations
\[E'=\frac{E}{1+\frac{E}{m_ec^2}(1-\cos\theta)},\qquad T_e=E-E'\]

\(E\) and \(E'\) are incident and scattered photon energies, \(m_ec^2\) is the electron rest energy, \(\theta\) is the photon angle, and \(T_e\) is recoil-electron energy.

\[E_{\mathrm{threshold}}=2m_ec^2=1.022\ \mathrm{MeV}\]

Pair production can occur above this threshold; the remaining energy is shared by the electron, positron, and recoil products.

Spectral hardening

A shield generally removes the lower-energy components of a mixed photon spectrum more effectively than the higher-energy components. Consequently, the transmitted spectrum becomes progressively enriched in high-energy photons. The effective attenuation coefficient and effective HVL therefore change with depth, even though each monoenergetic component follows its own attenuation behaviour.

Mixture equations
\[T_N=\sum_iw_iT_{N,i},\qquad T_E=\frac{\sum_iw_iE_iT_{E,i}}{\sum_iw_iE_i}\]

\(w_i\), \(E_i\), \(T_{N,i}\), and \(T_{E,i}\) are the source weight, energy, photon transmission, and energy transmission of component \(i\).

\[\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=-\frac{\ln[I(x_2)/I(x_1)]}{x_2-x_1}\]

The effective coefficient is an interval quantity for the mixed spectrum.

This section examines whether the arrangement of shielding layers changes the transmitted radiation. For photons that cross the shield without interacting, the layer order is irrelevant because the attenuation exponents simply add. The complete transmitted spectrum can nevertheless change because scattering and characteristic X rays are produced at different positions inside the shield. Comparing the same materials in reverse order isolates this effect.

Multilayer equation
\[T_{\mathrm{unc}}=\exp\left[-\sum_i\mu_ix_i\right]=\exp\left[-\sum_i\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)_im_{A,i}\right]\]

\(x_i\) and \(m_{A,i}\) are the thickness and areal density of layer \(i\). This expression applies to uncollided photons; the complete spectrum can still depend on layer order.

Charged particles

This section uses stopping-power and CSDA-range data rather than OpenMC charged-particle transport. The mass range removes the direct effect of material density and is useful for comparing materials, but it is not strictly material-independent because stopping power also depends on composition. Dividing the mass range by density gives an estimate of the corresponding physical range.

CSDA range comparison from local NIST data

The local energy-deposition model (ESTAR) and thick-target bremsstrahlung model (TTB) are used only where their data are available.

Graded-\(Z\) ordering

Source → low-\(Z\) → high-\(Z\) usually places the lower bremsstrahlung producer first, then attenuates photons farther out.

Source → high-\(Z\) → low-\(Z\) produces more bremsstrahlung close to the source. This qualitative comparison is not a transport result.

Charged-particle equations
\[R_m=\int_0^{E_0}\frac{\mathrm dE}{S(E)/\rho},\qquad R=\frac{R_m}{\rho}\]

\(R_m\) is mass CSDA range, \(R\) is physical range, \(S/\rho\) is mass stopping power, and \(\rho\) is material density.

\[Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\sum_in_iZ_i^2}{\sum_in_iZ_i}\]

This approximate effective atomic number depends on the process being considered.
