How to read these plots

Begin with the uncollided curve. If the OpenMC transmission lies above it, scattered or secondary photons are contributing to the downstream current. The buildup ratio quantifies that difference for this geometry; it is not an intrinsic property of the material.

The spectrum is reported as transmitted photons per source photon in each energy bin. It shows how the source spectrum is redistributed, but it is not an absolute flux unless a source rate and an area are specified.

Engineering implication. A shield selected from narrow beam attenuation alone can underestimate the radiation field outside a real barrier, especially when scattered photons can reach the point of interest.

Question for discussion. At what thickness does buildup become important, and does the answer change with material or photon energy?

Physical model and equations
\[\mu(E)=\rho\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)(E)\]

Here \(\mu\) is the linear attenuation coefficient, \(\rho\) is density, and \(\mu/\rho\) is the mass attenuation coefficient at photon energy \(E\).

\[m_A=\rho x,\qquad \tau=\mu x=\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)m_A\]

The physical thickness is \(x\), \(m_A\) is areal density, and \(\tau\) is optical thickness.

\[T_{\mathrm{unc}}(x)=e^{-\mu x},\qquad \mathrm{HVL}=\frac{\ln 2}{\mu},\qquad \mathrm{TVL}=\frac{\ln 10}{\mu}\]

The uncollided transmission, half-value layer, and tenth-value layer describe photons that do not interact.

\[T_N=\frac{J_{N,\mathrm{out}}}{J_{N,\mathrm{in}}},\qquad T_E=\frac{J_{E,\mathrm{out}}}{E_0J_{N,\mathrm{in}}}\]

\(T_N\) is the number of photons crossing the downstream surface per emitted source photon. \(T_E\) is the transmitted photon energy divided by the initial photon energy.

\[\overline{E}_{\mathrm{out}}=E_0\frac{T_E}{T_N},\qquad B_N=\frac{T_N}{T_{\mathrm{unc}}},\qquad B_E=\frac{T_E}{T_{\mathrm{unc}}}\]

\(\overline{E}_{\mathrm{out}}\) is the mean transmitted photon energy. \(B_N\) and \(B_E\) are specific to this geometry and tally response; they are not universal material properties.

Compare materials

Shielding materials can be compared using physical thickness, mass per unit area, or number of mean free paths. These comparisons answer different engineering questions. Equal physical thickness emphasizes compactness, while equal areal density compares shields having the same mass per unit area. Equal areal density does not imply equal attenuation because the mass attenuation coefficient depends on material composition and photon energy.

Material comparison at the selected basis

Interpreting a material comparison

Equal centimetres, equal areal density, and equal optical thickness are three different comparisons. Equal centimetres asks which shield is most compact. Equal areal density asks what can be achieved with the same shield mass per unit area. Equal optical thickness removes the difference in uncollided attenuation and makes the contribution from scattering easier to see.

Engineering implication. The material with the smallest physical thickness is not automatically the best design. Space, mass, cost, mechanical support, toxicity, secondary radiation, and ease of fabrication may lead to a different choice.

Photon interactions

The relative importance of Compton scattering, photoelectric absorption, and pair production changes with photon energy and material composition. The plotted quantities describe the probability that the first interaction is of each type. They do not describe the final fate of the complete photon history, which may contain several successive interactions.

Probability of the first photon interaction

What controls the interaction probabilities?

Photoelectric absorption is favoured at lower photon energies and in materials with high atomic number. Compton scattering dominates a broad intermediate energy range and depends mainly on electron density. Pair production becomes possible only above 1.022 MeV and grows in importance as energy and atomic number increase.

These curves identify the first interaction only. A Compton event does not remove the photon; it changes its direction and energy and may be followed by further interactions.

Engineering implication. High atomic number materials are effective absorbers, but photoelectric interactions can generate characteristic X rays. The secondary spectrum, not only the total attenuation coefficient, matters when selecting layers.

Compton and pair-production equations
\[E'=\frac{E}{1+\frac{E}{m_ec^2}(1-\cos\theta)},\qquad T_e=E-E'\]

\(E\) and \(E'\) are incident and scattered photon energies, \(m_ec^2\) is the electron rest energy, \(\theta\) is the photon angle, and \(T_e\) is recoil-electron energy.

\[E_{\mathrm{threshold}}=2m_ec^2=1.022\ \mathrm{MeV}\]

Pair production can occur above this threshold; the remaining energy is shared by the electron, positron, and recoil products.

Spectral hardening

A shield generally removes the lower-energy components of a mixed photon spectrum more effectively than the higher-energy components. Consequently, the transmitted spectrum becomes progressively enriched in high-energy photons. The effective attenuation coefficient and effective HVL therefore change with depth, even though each monoenergetic component follows its own attenuation behaviour.

Fraction of transmitted photons from the 1 MeV component

Why the effective HVL changes

Spectral hardening is a property of a mixed spectrum. Lower energy components are preferentially removed, so the photons that remain are, on average, more penetrating. A monoenergetic beam does not harden in this sense, although scattering can still populate lower energy bins.

Engineering implication. A single HVL measured near the shield entrance should not be extrapolated indefinitely through a thick barrier when the source is polyenergetic. The transmitted spectrum and the effective attenuation coefficient evolve with depth.

Question for discussion. Over which thickness interval would you report an effective HVL for this spectrum?

Mixture equations
\[T_N=\sum_iw_iT_{N,i},\qquad T_E=\frac{\sum_iw_iE_iT_{E,i}}{\sum_iw_iE_i}\]

\(w_i\), \(E_i\), \(T_{N,i}\), and \(T_{E,i}\) are the source weight, energy, photon transmission, and energy transmission of component \(i\).

\[\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=-\frac{\ln[I(x_2)/I(x_1)]}{x_2-x_1}\]

The effective coefficient is an interval quantity for the mixed spectrum.

Reading the layer comparison

The uncollided transmission is unchanged when the same layers are reversed because the attenuation exponents add. Any difference between configurations A and B therefore comes from the transport of scattered and secondary photons. Compare the difference with its uncertainty before assigning physical meaning to a small separation.

Engineering implication. Layer order should be chosen for the secondary spectrum that must be controlled. There is no universal ordering that is optimal for every photon energy, material combination, and detector location.

Multilayer equation
\[T_{\mathrm{unc}}=\exp\left[-\sum_i\mu_ix_i\right]=\exp\left[-\sum_i\left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)_im_{A,i}\right]\]

\(x_i\) and \(m_{A,i}\) are the thickness and areal density of layer \(i\). This expression applies to uncollided photons; the complete spectrum can still depend on layer order.

Charged particles

This section uses stopping-power and CSDA-range data rather than OpenMC charged-particle transport. The mass range removes the direct effect of material density and is useful for comparing materials, but it is not strictly material-independent because stopping power also depends on composition. Dividing the mass range by density gives an estimate of the corresponding physical range.

CSDA range comparison from local NIST data

The local energy-deposition model (ESTAR) and thick-target bremsstrahlung model (TTB) are used only where their data are available.

Graded-\(Z\) ordering

Source → low-\(Z\) → high-\(Z\) usually places the lower bremsstrahlung producer first, then attenuates photons farther out.

Source → high-\(Z\) → low-\(Z\) produces more bremsstrahlung close to the source. This qualitative comparison is not a transport result.

Range is not the same as penetration depth

The CSDA range is obtained by integrating the inverse stopping power along the particle path. For alpha particles the path is nearly straight, so range and penetration depth are close. Electron tracks are much more tortuous because of multiple scattering, and their maximum depth is smaller than their total path length.

Engineering implication. A thin layer may stop the primary charged particles but still leave a photon shielding problem. This is particularly important for energetic electrons in high atomic number materials, where bremsstrahlung production is enhanced.

Question for discussion. Why can replacing a low atomic number electron shield with a thinner lead layer increase the radiation field outside the shield?

Charged-particle equations
\[R_m=\int_0^{E_0}\frac{\mathrm dE}{S(E)/\rho},\qquad R=\frac{R_m}{\rho}\]

\(R_m\) is mass CSDA range, \(R\) is physical range, \(S/\rho\) is mass stopping power, and \(\rho\) is material density.

\[Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\sum_in_iZ_i^2}{\sum_in_iZ_i}\]

This approximate effective atomic number depends on the process being considered.
